About the Roman Numeral Converter

The Roman Numeral Converter turns a number into Roman numerals and reads Roman numerals back into numbers. Type either form and the other appears.

The system uses seven symbols: I for one, V for five, X for ten, L for fifty, C for a hundred, D for five hundred and M for a thousand. Values are normally written largest first and added, but six subtractive pairs are allowed to keep numerals short: IV for four, IX for nine, XL for forty, XC for ninety, CD for four hundred and CM for nine hundred.

The standard form covers one to 3,999. Beyond that, the conventional notation runs out of symbols, since M repeats without a dedicated sign for five thousand. Clock faces traditionally use IIII rather than IV for four, which is a stylistic exception rather than a rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the converter stop at 3,999?

Standard Roman notation has no symbol above M, so larger numbers need extra marks such as a bar over a letter, which is outside the usual system.

How does subtractive notation work?

A smaller symbol before a larger one subtracts: IV is four, IX is nine, XL is forty, XC is ninety, CD is four hundred, CM is nine hundred.

Is IIII ever correct?

On some clock faces, yes, by tradition. In normal writing four is IV.

Is there a Roman numeral for zero?

No. The system has no zero; it counts positions rather than quantities with a placeholder.

Can I enter lowercase letters?

Yes, i, v and x are read the same as capitals.

Why do some years look different from others?

Because there are several conventions for large numbers. This tool uses the standard form used for years and dates.
