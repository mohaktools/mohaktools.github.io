About the SIP Calculator

The SIP Calculator projects what a monthly systematic investment plan could grow to. Enter the monthly amount, the annual return you expect and the number of years, and it returns the total you put in, the estimated returns and the maturity value.

The formula treats each instalment as invested at the start of its month, so it compounds for the full month: maturity equals the monthly amount, multiplied by ((1 + i)^n - 1) / i, multiplied by (1 + i), where i is the monthly rate and n the number of instalments. That extra (1 + i) is the difference between this and an ordinary annuity, and it is worth roughly one month's growth on the final value.

The return is an assumption, not a promise. Twelve per cent is a common long-run figure for Indian equity funds, but actual returns vary by year and by fund, and a bad decade is possible. The result also ignores expense ratios, exit loads and tax on gains, all of which reduce what you actually receive.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is the SIP maturity value calculated?

Maturity = monthly amount x ((1 + i)^n - 1) / i x (1 + i), where i is the monthly rate of return and n the number of monthly instalments.

Why is there a (1 + i) at the end?

It assumes each instalment is invested at the start of the month and so earns a full month's return. Investing at month end would drop that factor.

What return should I assume?

Twelve per cent is a common long-run assumption for Indian equity funds. Debt funds have historically been lower. Treat any figure as a scenario, not a forecast.

Are returns guaranteed?

No. Mutual fund returns depend on the market and are not fixed. The calculator shows what a steady return would produce, which real markets never deliver evenly.

Does it account for expense ratio or exit load?

No. Both reduce your actual return. A 1% expense ratio alone can take a noticeable bite out of a long-term corpus.

Can I model a yearly increase in my SIP?

Not here. A step-up SIP needs each year's instalments calculated at a different amount. You can approximate by running the years separately and adding the results.
