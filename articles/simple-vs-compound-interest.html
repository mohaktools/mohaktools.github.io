Simple vs Compound Interest: Difference, Formula and Example

Finance basics · Published 6 December 2026

Interest can work for you when you save, or against you when you borrow. This guide explains the difference between simple and compound interest, shows both formulas, and compares them with one clear example.

Whether you are opening a savings account, taking a loan, or comparing investment options, you will see the words simple interest and compound interest. They sound similar, but the way they grow money is very different. Understanding this difference helps you pick the better option.

What is simple interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount, called the principal. The interest you earn or pay does not get added back into the principal, so it does not earn more interest. The amount grows in a straight line over time.

Simple interest formula: SI = P × r × t

Where P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, and t is the time in years.

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus the interest that has already been added. In other words, you earn interest on your interest. Over short periods the difference is small, but over many years it becomes large. This is why compound interest is often called the most powerful force in saving.

Compound interest formula: A = P × (1 + r/n)n×t

Here A is the final amount, P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is how many times interest is added per year, and t is the number of years. If interest is added once a year, n = 1.

Example: ₹10,000 at 10% for 3 years

Let's compare both methods using the same numbers: a principal of ₹10,000, an interest rate of 10% per year, and a period of 3 years.

Simple interest

SI = 10,000 × 0.10 × 3 = ₹3,000. The total amount after 3 years is ₹13,000.

Compound interest (added yearly)

A = 10,000 × (1 + 0.10)3 = 10,000 × 1.331 = ₹13,310. The interest earned is ₹3,310.

The difference is ₹310 after just 3 years. The gap widens over longer periods. Try the same numbers over 10 years and the compound amount becomes much larger than the simple amount.

Compare the two side by side

  • Growth: simple interest grows in a straight line; compound interest grows faster over time.
  • Base: simple interest uses only the principal; compound interest uses principal plus past interest.
  • Best for: simple interest is common in short-term loans and some personal loans; compound interest is common in savings accounts, fixed deposits, and investments.
  • Frequency: compound interest can be added yearly, half-yearly, quarterly, or monthly. More frequent compounding means slightly more growth.

Which one is better for you?

The answer depends on which side of the transaction you are on.

  • When you save or invest, compound interest is better because your money grows faster.
  • When you borrow, simple interest is usually better because you pay less total interest. Always check whether the loan uses simple or compound interest before you sign.

Common mistakes

Using the percentage as a whole number

In the formulas, 10% must be written as 0.10. If you put 10 instead, your answer will be 100 times too large.

Forgetting the compounding frequency

A savings account that compounds monthly will grow more than one that compounds yearly, even at the same rate. Always check how often interest is added.

Mixing up time units

If the rate is annual, the time must be in years. For 18 months, use t = 1.5.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount. Compound interest is calculated on the original amount plus the interest already earned, so it grows faster over time.

What is the formula for compound interest?

A = P × (1 + r/n)n×t, where P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years.

Which is better, simple or compound interest?

Compound interest is better when you are earning on savings or investments. Simple interest is usually better when you are paying interest on a loan.

Conclusion

Simple interest adds a fixed amount each year, while compound interest adds growth on top of growth. Use compound interest to your advantage when saving, and check for simple interest when borrowing.
