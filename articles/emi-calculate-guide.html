How to Calculate EMI: Formula, Example and Free Calculator

Calculators · Published 15 November 2026

Taking a loan means paying a fixed amount every month. This guide explains what EMI is, how the formula works, and how to calculate it by hand with a real example.

When you take a home loan, car loan, or personal loan, the lender usually asks you to repay it in equal monthly payments. That fixed amount is called EMI, short for Equated Monthly Instalment. Knowing your EMI before you sign helps you plan your monthly budget and compare loan offers fairly.

In this article, you will learn what EMI means, the formula behind it, a step-by-step example, and the mistakes people commonly make. At the end, you can check your own numbers with our free EMI calculator.

What is EMI?

EMI is the amount you pay to the bank every month until the loan is fully repaid. Each EMI has two parts: the principal (the money you borrowed) and the interest (the cost of borrowing). In the early months, a bigger share of your EMI goes towards interest. As the loan reduces, more of it goes towards the principal.

The EMI formula

The standard formula for EMI is:

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n / ((1 + r)n − 1)

Where:

  • P is the principal, or the loan amount.
  • r is the monthly interest rate. If the annual rate is 10%, then r = 10 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.00833.
  • n is the number of monthly instalments. A 5-year loan has n = 60.

The formula looks complicated, but you only need to plug in three numbers. Let's go through a full example so you can see how it works.

Step-by-step example

Suppose you take a personal loan of ₹10,00,000 at 10% annual interest for 5 years. Here is how to find the EMI.

Step 1: Note down the three values

  • P = ₹10,00,000
  • Annual interest = 10%, so monthly r = 10 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.008333
  • Tenure = 5 years, so n = 5 × 12 = 60 months

Step 2: Calculate (1 + r)n

(1 + 0.008333)60 is approximately 1.6453. You can calculate this on a scientific calculator, or use the tool linked below to skip this step.

Step 3: Put the values into the formula

EMI = 10,00,000 × 0.008333 × 1.6453 / (1.6453 − 1)

That gives an EMI of about ₹21,247 per month.

Step 4: Check the total cost

Over 60 months, you will pay ₹21,247 × 60 = ₹12,74,820 in total. Out of this, ₹2,74,820 is interest. This is the real cost of borrowing ₹10 lakh for 5 years at 10%.

What happens if you change the tenure?

The tenure has a big effect on your monthly payment. Using the same ₹10,00,000 at 10%:

  • 3 years (36 months): EMI is about ₹32,267. Total interest is about ₹1,61,612.
  • 5 years (60 months): EMI is about ₹21,247. Total interest is about ₹2,74,820.
  • 7 years (84 months): EMI is about ₹16,122. Total interest is about ₹3,54,248.

A longer tenure gives a smaller EMI, which feels easier on your monthly budget. But you pay much more interest in the end. Choose the shortest tenure you can comfortably afford.

Common mistakes to avoid

Using the annual rate directly

The formula needs the monthly rate, not the annual rate. If you put 10 in place of 0.00833, your answer will be far too high. Always divide the annual rate by 12 and by 100.

Mixing up months and years

A 5-year loan is 60 instalments, not 5. Using years instead of months will give a wrong result.

Ignoring processing fees and other charges

The EMI formula only covers principal and interest. Processing fees, insurance, and penalties are separate. Read the loan agreement to know the full cost.

Forgetting that the rate can be floating

Many home loans have a floating rate, which can change over time. Your EMI may go up or down. The calculation above shows a fixed-rate example.

Frequently asked questions

What is EMI?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment. It is the fixed amount you pay every month to repay a loan, including both principal and interest.

What is the formula for EMI?

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n / ((1 + r)n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of monthly instalments.

Does a longer loan tenure reduce EMI?

Yes. A longer tenure lowers your monthly EMI, but you pay more total interest over the life of the loan.
